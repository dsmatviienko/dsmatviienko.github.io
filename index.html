Research

Regulatory Capture and Superstars: Lobbying, Innovation, and Industry Dynamics

Job market paper · with Pedro Oliboni · draft coming soon
Abstract

Corporate lobbying serves two distinct goals: increasing government transfers for the firm itself through tax provisions, procurement and research credits, and shaping the regulatory requirements that set the fixed cost of operating for every firm in the industry. They have different implications for competition, innovation, and welfare, and firms in practice do both. We classify the universe of federal lobbying reports filed by firms and trade associations, together with the bills they cite, into a complete taxonomy of economic policy instruments, recording for each issue the instrument at stake, the direction the filer wants it to move, and the firms it applies to. Lobbying for firm-specific instruments follows exposure: profits predict lobbying to lower the corporate income tax, research spending predicts lobbying to raise the research credit, and sales to the government predict lobbying on appropriations and procurement. Lobbying over regulation is contested instead: within an industry, larger firms lobby to raise requirements while smaller firms, mostly through trade associations, lobby to lower them. We embed both kinds of lobbying in a dynamic general equilibrium model of oligopolistic competition and innovation in which regulation is decided by a contest between large incumbents and the fringe. In the model, most large incumbents favour higher fixed costs: leaders because marginal fringe firms exit and their share rises, and smaller among large firms because the costs deter the entrants that would displace them. Calibrated to US data, lobbying is costly mainly because it lowers long-run growth. The loss arises through industry composition: higher regulatory fixed costs leave each surviving firm innovating more, but make industries more concentrated, with fewer large firms, so that innovation per industry and in the aggregate falls. Banning lobbying over regulation removes most of the welfare loss, while banning lobbying for firm-specific transfers removes almost none of it.

Household Heterogeneity and Mortgage Default

with Augustus Kmetz · working paper
Abstract

Borrowers who face similar shocks default at very different rates. We explore how much of that dispersion reflects ex ante heterogeneity in preferences of households rather than the realization of income and house-price risk. To answer this question, we estimate a life-cycle model of housing, mortgage choice, saving, and default in which households differ ex ante in patience, elasticity of substitution, and permanent income. We use PSID microdata and the simulated method of moments to estimate the model over the realized housing boom and bust. Preference heterogeneity is essential to matching the data: a single-type version of the model cannot reproduce the hand-to-mouth gradient in default, the consumption response to hand-to-mouth status, and the timing of the aggregate foreclosure spike during the financial crisis. We find that most defaults come from impatient households, who are also persistently hand-to-mouth. We also confirm the finding from the literature that defaults follow income declines and mostly occur with positive home equity: foreclosure is driven by a liquidity shock; it’s not a strategic choice driven by negative equity. In addition to decomposing the contribution of preferences in the aggregate foreclosure rate, we find that targeted liquidity provision to households based on a history of hand-to-mouth status can increase the effectiveness of policies similar to the Homeowner Assistance Fund.

Green Innovation, Environmental Policy, and Sustainable Investing

solo-authored · draft available on request
Abstract

Macroeconomic research on the green transition and finance research on sustainable investing—by funds, individual investors, and central banks through green-asset purchases—have developed largely in isolation. This paper connects them by asking how a higher cost of capital affects firms that produce and innovate with both clean and dirty technologies. Evidence shows that higher capital costs for brown firms can increase pollution, and that dirty firms produce many high-quality green innovations. I develop a parsimonious perfect-foresight general-equilibrium model with directed technical change and multiproduct firms. Dirty-input taxes and clean research subsidies reduce emissions, but a higher cost of capital amplifies the existing direction of technical change: when dirty innovation is relatively more productive, firms shift further toward it, which can slow the green transition. ESG strategies and central-bank green-asset purchases should therefore account not only for emissions intensity, but also for the substitutability of clean and dirty production and the structure of firms' innovation.

The Optimal Level of Government Debt and Wealth Inequality

solo-authored · Best Master's Thesis Prize, Economist Club Luxembourg (2020)
Abstract

This paper studies the optimal level of US government debt in a continuous-time heterogeneous-agent model with incomplete markets, portfolio choice, and aggregate disaster risk, extending Aiyagari and McGrattan (1998). The income process uses autoregressive dynamics with normal-mixture innovations to match the observed income distribution. Disaster risk calls for a fiscal buffer, so the maximum welfare-improving debt-to-GDP ratio is lower than in models without it. The optimal US debt-to-GDP ratio is between 120% and 140%.
